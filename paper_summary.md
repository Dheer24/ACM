A Strategic Discipline of Inquiry: “How to Read a Paper.”

In How to Read a Paper, S. Keshav “three-pass approach,” an elegant, time-saving paradigm for not getting sucked under the waves of detailed research. 

Instead of reading word-for-word from end to end-an exhausting process-Keshav provides a time-tiered system: (1) a rapid 5–10min “Pass” to skim the abstract, headlines, and conclusion and gain an overall sense of the paper’s research area and its main thrust; (2) a second “Pass” to review key sections and figures and develop a holistic narrative; and (3) a critical, final “Pass” where the reader should endeavor to (mentally) re-implement the work to expose underlying assumptions, judge the experimental setup, and detect methodological shortcomings. 

By far the most valuable takeaway was the license to not understand everything the first time around. ‘Pass One’ allows you to reframe your goal from “immediate mastery” to “high-priority triage,” guiding you through five quick checks (Category, Context, Correctness, Contributions, Clarity-the ‘five Cs’) to decide whether it warrants your limited bandwidth for Passes Two and Three. I also wholeheartedly identified with the advice of Pass Two to look critically and aggressively at figures and axes (often they’re telling the simplest/most honest stories). Whenever I engage with higher-level computer engineering topics for a tech committee or for my more complicated algorithms-based projects, I almost invariably trip and fall into the thicket. 

I look forward to adopting this strategy immediately next time I pick up one of those long papers with complicated mathematical derivations on computer vision/deep learning-y things. 

I'll hold myself accountable to staying only ten minutes on Pass One and letting my curiosity drive decisions on whether to delve further, saving many hours and improving focus with far sharper critical engagement.
